Decoding the Danes

Direct, not rude

Danes say what they mean, especially in group work. A “no” is information, not an insult.

“I don’t think that works.” = I don’t think that works.

Flat hierarchy

First names for everyone, professors included. You’re expected to speak up and disagree.

“Hej Lars!” → said to your professor

On time means on time

Lectures, group meetings and dinner invites start exactly when they say.

“See you at 16:30” → be there 16:29

Janteloven

The unwritten rule: don’t think you’re better than anyone. Modesty is cool. Bragging is not.

“It went okay” = I got a 12

Directness: feedback like “I don’t think that works” is about the work, not about you. Flat hierarchy: first names for teachers, everyone’s opinion counts. Punctuality: being late to a group meeting gets noticed. Janteloven: don’t show off about money or grades. 12 is the top grade on the Danish scale, so “it went okay” can mean you aced it. Hygge is a survival strategy

Candles, blankets, coffee, good people. When it’s dark at four, Danes don’t hide. They get cosy together. If someone invites you for hygge, say yes.

Watch the sun: on the shortest day it rises around 08:37 and sets around 15:38. Many internationals find November–February the hardest months. Hygge is how Danes cope: small cosy gatherings, candles, coffee and cake. Tips: get outside in daylight, take vitamin D, stay active, accept invitations. A good rain jacket beats an umbrella because the wind destroys umbrellas.

Housing is the number-one stress. S.dk and Kollegiernes Kontor are the two big portals for dorms (kollegier). findbolig.nu runs waiting lists, so sign up early because seniority counts. BoligPortal costs money but has many private listings. Facebook groups are fast but full of scams. Press → to reveal the red flags one by one, then the stamp. Never pay before you’ve seen the place and signed a contract. Danish law caps the deposit at 3 months’ rent (plus up to 3 months prepaid rent). Ambassadors: tell how you found your place.

Adulting in Denmark, in five steps

1

CPR number

Your Danish ID number. It unlocks everything else.

2

Yellow card

Your health card, with your own doctor on it.

3

MitID

Your login for banks, CBS and the state.

4

NemKonto

A Danish bank account linked to your CPR.

5

Digital Post

Official letters land here. Check it often.

20 h

Non-EU students: your permit normally lets you work 20 hours a week during the semester and full-time in June, July and August. Always check your own permit on nyidanmark.dk.

Order matters: the CPR number (International House or your municipality) comes first. With it you get the yellow health card with your own GP, and healthcare is free. MitID is the digital ID for almost everything. NemKonto is the account the state and employers pay into. Digital Post is where official letters arrive; missing them can mean fines. Non-EU work rules: 20 hours a week in the semester, full-time in the summer months. Working more can cost you your residence permit.

Bike like a Dane

Hand up

Means you’re about to stop.

Arm out

Point left or right before you turn.

Lights on

After dark, or the police will fine you.

Slow? Keep right

Fast riders pass on the left.

Lock it. Always.

Even for two minutes.

Buy used

DBA.dk, Facebook Marketplace, or rent from Swapfiets.

Biking is how Copenhagen moves, and Danish cyclists are fast and strict. Hand up = stopping, arm out = turning. Lights after dark are required and the police do give fines. Slow riders keep right. Never stop suddenly in the bike lane. Bike theft is common, so always lock it. Second-hand: DBA.dk or Facebook Marketplace. Swapfiets is a monthly bike subscription if you want zero hassle.

What actually helped us

  • Join a student organisation. The fastest way to Danish friends.
  • Go to Friday bars. Same faces every week turn into friends.
  • Say yes. To dinners, study groups and random invites.
  • Learn a little Danish. Even “tak” gets you smiles.
  • Build a routine. A sport, a café, a weekly call home.

Your first Danish winter

J-Dag is in 9 days Friday 6 November, 20:59. The Christmas beer arrives.
  • 6 NovJ-DagSanta hats, blue tinsel and free Christmas beer in the bars.
  • Mid-NovTivoli ChristmasLights, gløgg and æbleskiver in the city centre.
  • DecemberJulefrokostThe “Christmas lunch” that somehow lasts all night.
  • 24 DecChristmas EveThe big day. Danes hold hands and dance around the tree.
  • 31 DecNytårFireworks everywhere. Jump off a chair at midnight.
Something to look forward to in the dark months. J-Dag is the first Friday of November, this year Friday 6 November: Tuborg’s Christmas beer is released at 20:59 and bars get very busy. Tivoli opens its Christmas season around mid-November. If classmates invite you to a julefrokost, go! Danes celebrate Christmas on the evening of the 24th. On New Year’s Eve people watch the King’s speech, eat kransekage, and jump into the new year from a chair. Not going home for Christmas? Ask around, many people invite others in.
